**The honest answer for a skeptical business owner in 2026: somewhere between fifteen and thirty percent of high-intent local-services search behavior has already migrated to AI assistants, and that share is still growing roughly two to three points per quarter. That's enough to matter for revenue but not yet enough to be the dominant channel. Treating AEO as either a fad to ignore or a complete replacement for local SEO would both be wrong. The right frame is: it's a new channel growing fast enough that the cost of starting late will be meaningfully higher than the cost of starting now, but the urgency case isn't "or you'll go out of business" — it's "or you'll spend two years catching up on something that took six months to build."**

The skepticism is reasonable. Marketing has produced enough false-emergency claims over the last decade that "the next thing" gets a built-in discount from anyone who's been operating a small business for more than five years. AEO deserves a more honest accounting than the breathless takes give it.

## What's actually happening in the data

The clearest signal isn't surveys of consumer intent — those have always been noisy and self-reported. The clearest signal is the volume of zero-click search behavior, which is what happens when a buyer gets their answer from an AI summary and never clicks through to a website.

Major studies in 2025 and early 2026 put zero-click rates for local-services queries between 25 and 40 percent depending on category. That doesn't all flow through AI assistants — some of it is Google's own answer boxes pulling from featured snippets, some is map-pack visibility without click-through. But a meaningful slice of that zero-click traffic is AI-assistant-mediated answers where the buyer made a decision without ever visiting a contractor's website.

The other signal: AI assistant usage among the 35-to-54 demographic — the prime homeowner-spending bracket — grew roughly 4x from early 2024 to early 2026. Not 4x of a small base, either; this demographic is now the highest-frequency AI-assistant user group, ahead of even college-age users.

## What that means for revenue, concretely

For a $5M-revenue local services business with healthy growth, the math typically lands like this:

- Roughly 70 percent of revenue is referral-and-repeat — unaffected by AI search in the near term.
- Roughly 30 percent is acquisition-driven — split across Google search, Google maps, paid media, and (now) AI-assistant recommendations.
- Of that 30 percent, AI-mediated discovery in 2026 likely accounts for 15 to 25 percent — call it 5 percent of total revenue.

Five percent of revenue isn't catastrophic to lose, but it's also not negligible — for our example business, that's $250K of annual revenue that's now contestable in a channel the business may not be competing in at all. And the share is growing.

The relevant question isn't "is AEO existential" — it's "is the cost of capturing that 5 percent meaningfully lower today than it will be in 18 months when the share is closer to 12 percent."

## What's hype and what's structural

A useful split:

### Hype (discount these claims)

- "AI search will replace Google by 2027" — no current evidence supports this; Google's traffic share is essentially flat year-over-year and may stabilize at current levels for years.
- "If you're not first in AI answers, you're invisible" — for high-consideration purchases, buyers still validate with multiple sources, including direct search and review platforms.
- "You need to dominate every AI assistant" — most buyers use one or two; covering ChatGPT and Google AI Overview captures the largest share with the least effort.

### Structural (these aren't going away)

- AI-assistant-mediated answers are now part of the buyer journey for high-consideration local-services purchases — even when they're not the final touchpoint.
- The 35-to-65 demographic — the buying demographic for home services — has adopted AI assistants at higher rates than younger users would have predicted in 2024.
- Models cite based on retrievable signals; signals are slower to build than they are to maintain. Late entrants pay more for the same position.
- Local SEO and AEO share some signals (entity consistency, reviews, schema) but diverge meaningfully on others (passage-shaped content, citation share monitoring, FAQ depth).

## The skeptic's defensible position

If you're inclined to wait, here's the defensible version of that position:

You can probably wait six to twelve months before starting AEO work and still be in reasonable shape *if* — and only if — three conditions hold:

1. Your local SEO is already solid (GBP optimized, schema in place, reviews flowing, NAP consistent).
2. Your buyer demographics skew older than the 35-to-54 prime AI-assistant bracket.
3. You're in a category where buyers do significant offline research before purchase (high-trust trades like custom home building, specialty medical).

If those three don't all apply, the cost of waiting compounds. Not catastrophically — but enough that 12 months from now you'll be doing the same work at a higher price, against more competitors who started sooner.

## The smarter framing

Stop asking whether AEO is a fad. The honest answer is "no, but it's also not the apocalypse." Start asking: what's the lowest-cost foundation we can build right now that captures the structural shift, doesn't bet the farm on the hype, and gives us optionality if the share grows faster than expected?

That foundation is well-defined: optimize the GBP, fix the website's schema and entity consistency, deploy answer-shaped FAQ content for your most-asked buyer questions, set up citation monitoring on a small prompt set, and review quarterly. That's not a $5K/month engagement — it's a $1K to $2K/month engagement that hedges the structural risk without overcommitting to the hype.

The agencies pitching the apocalypse will tell you that's not enough. They're wrong. The right size of bet on AEO in 2026 is "meaningful, not heroic." That's the bet most local services businesses should make — and the one that pencils out whether the hype proves right or wrong.
